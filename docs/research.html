Time to Weight Loss and Subsequent Weight Maintenance: A Survival Analysis

In brief: In an 18-month randomized trial, financial incentives raised the rate of reaching 5% weight loss by about 37% at any given time, with larger gains for people with higher starting BMI. People who became fast weight losers only because of the incentives kept the weight off longer.

Show abstractHide abstract

This study explores the timing of weight loss using time-to-event (survival) analysis and data from a randomized controlled trial (RCT) of a weight control program. The trial includes 418 participants (206 in the treatment group and 212 in the control group) across two clinical sites (South Carolina and Vermont) and has two main phases: an initial weight loss phase (months 1 to 6) and a weight maintenance phase (months 7 to 18). Participants randomized into the treatment group received financial incentives based on their behaviors and outcomes during the study.

For the weight loss phase, we investigate whether, and by how much, financial incentives reduce the time needed to achieve weight loss (a 5% weight reduction), and which participants benefit most from the incentives. For the weight maintenance phase, we examine whether participants who lost weight more quickly achieved better long-term outcomes. Our analytical framework combines several complementary causal inference approaches to address identification challenges. We use Cox models to estimate average treatment effects, causal survival forests to identify individual-level treatment effects, and principal stratification to estimate the effect of the time to weight loss on the duration of weight maintenance.

Our findings show that financial incentives increase the rate of achieving weight loss by approximately 37% at any given time, with substantial differences in treatment effects across participants. Participants with higher baseline BMI show systematically larger benefits from financial incentives. Our weight maintenance analysis shows that participants who become “fast weight losers” only under treatment conditions experience significantly longer maintenance benefits.

These findings offer practical recommendations for improving weight control programs. Health care systems should prioritize individuals with higher baseline BMI when offering financial incentives and design comprehensive interventions that include financial incentives.

An Exploratory Analysis of Patterns of Weight Change in a Post-Cessation Weight Management Trial

Nicotine & Tobacco Research, 2025. Journal page · [AI-Generated Podcast by NotebookLM]

Show abstractHide abstract

Using daily e-scale data from the “Fit & Quit” post-cessation weight management trial (n = 132), this study uses mixed-effects and polynomial models to describe how weight changes after people quit smoking. Weight gain begins immediately after quitting, peaks around day 21, and levels off by roughly day 70, with no differences by intervention condition or gender. The findings suggest that post-cessation weight management programs should focus on the first month after quitting.

Machine Learning-Driven Insights into the Structural and Socioeconomic Predictors of Top Five Cancer Mortality Rates in the U.S.

Presented at the American Public Health Association (APHA) Annual Meeting, November 2025

Show abstractHide abstract

This study uses 23 county-level structural and socioeconomic variables from 2018 to 2022 to compare machine learning models (Random Forest and Gradient Boosting) with traditional regression models (Linear, Ridge, and LASSO) in predicting mortality for all cancers combined and for the top five cancer sites. The machine learning models consistently outperform the linear models, and the main predictors differ meaningfully by cancer type. Disability and poverty indicators drive mortality from screenable cancers, while housing and internet access drive pancreatic cancer mortality. These results point to the need for public health strategies tailored to each cancer site.

The Role of Motivations in Weight Control Programs: An RCT Study

Presented at the 14th Annual Conference of the American Society of Health Economists (ASHEcon), June 2025 · [AI-Generated Podcast by NotebookLM]

Show abstractHide abstract

This study analyzes data from a randomized controlled trial of an 18-month behavioral weight control program to understand how financial incentives influence long-term weight outcomes through motivation. Participants (N = 418) were randomized into a control group (n = 212) and a treatment group (n = 206) taking part in an online program, with the treatment group receiving additional financial incentives for weight control behaviors and targets. Participants were classified in two phases: whether they achieved 5% weight loss at month 6 (weight loss vs. no weight loss), and whether they kept their weight below both baseline and month 6 levels at month 18 (weight maintenance vs. no weight maintenance).

Using the Treatment Self-Regulation Questionnaire, administered at baseline and at months 2, 6, 12, and 18, we apply sequential mediation analysis to trace how the intervention’s effects pass through early changes in motivation, weight loss success, later changes in motivation, and final weight maintenance. Financial incentives significantly reduced the early decline in intrinsic motivation, and each one-unit improvement in early motivation increased the probability of weight loss success by 9.0 percentage points. In turn, weight loss success strengthened later intrinsic motivation, which increased the probability of successful weight maintenance by 15.16 percentage points. We also find no evidence of crowding-out effects: financial incentives neither undermined intrinsic motivation nor caused greater weight regain among incentivized participants after the incentives ended.

These findings show that financial incentives work through both motivational and non-motivational channels, creating a chain of psychological effects that supports lasting weight control. Programs should assess intrinsic motivation early, give extra support to people with lower intrinsic motivation, and gradually reduce external incentives while strengthening intrinsic motivation to improve long-term success.

Working paperDissertation chapter

Incentive Design and Long-Term Weight Management Success: A Counterfactual Policy Analysis

Show abstractHide abstract

Financial incentives are widely used to promote weight loss, yet little is known about how incentive design, including the type, timing, and order of rewards, shapes long-term success. We apply sequential G-estimation of structural nested mean models to data from an 18-month randomized controlled trial of an online behavioral weight control program (N = 418) to evaluate incentive regimes that the trial did not directly randomize. We compare four population-level regimes: no incentives; the trial’s alternating schedule of behavioral-plus-outcome and outcome-only incentives; continuous outcome-only incentives; and continuous behavioral-plus-outcome incentives. We define long-term success as at least 5% weight loss from baseline sustained through 18 months.

Regimes that include behavioral incentives outperform outcome-only designs by roughly 7 to 8 percentage points, while extending behavioral incentives continuously improves only slightly on the trial’s alternating protocol, a gain not statistically different from zero. An exploratory adherence-weighted extension further suggests that sustained engagement during the program’s later behavioral-incentive phase predicts success much more strongly than early engagement. This indicates that long-term success requires adherence through the later phase, not early adherence alone. Although this finding is associational, it points to engagement, and not only incentive design, as a target for future work.

A cost-effectiveness framework that includes both incentive payments and fixed delivery costs shows that continuous behavioral incentives are the least cost-effective option on a cost-per-success basis, while continuous outcome-only incentives and the trial’s alternating protocol are the most cost-effective of the active regimes. These findings suggest that program administrators can achieve near-optimal outcomes at lower cost by combining behavioral-plus-outcome and outcome-only incentives in the trial’s alternating sequence, rather than extending behavioral incentives continuously. Further gains may come from strengthening engagement in the program’s later phase rather than from more generous incentives. These results offer practical guidance for scalable, cost-effective weight management programs that complement pharmacological treatment.

Working paper

Impact of Treatment Delays on Healthcare Costs in Patients with Breast Cancer: A Mediation Analysis of Healthcare Utilization by Socioeconomic Status

Accepted for presentation at ASHEcon 2026

Show abstractHide abstract

This study uses SEER-Medicare data from 2010 to 2019 on 74,173 breast cancer patients to examine how treatment delays, defined as more than two months from diagnosis to first treatment, affect health care use and costs, with results stratified by socioeconomic status (SES) using the Yost index. Mediation analysis shows that delays raise costs through greater health care use by $1,613.57 among high-SES patients and $1,891.15 among low-SES patients. Yet the total effect of delays on costs is negative, lowering overall spending by $2,192.79 and $981.59, respectively. This suggests that delays reflect missed or incomplete care and are a hidden driver of inequity and inefficiency.
